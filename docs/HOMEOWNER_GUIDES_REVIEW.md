# Bathroom renovation: plan the work before you pull anything out

Choose a cosmetic refresh or a full renovation, prepare a useful scope, compare estimates, and understand waterproofing, downtime, and the DIY tasks you can take on.

## What this project involves

A bathroom can look dated and still function well—or look fine while hiding moisture damage. Start by deciding what needs to change: appearance, storage, accessibility, leaks, ventilation, or layout. A new paint color and cabinet pulls are a different project from replacing a shower or moving a toilet. This guide helps you separate those scopes before buying materials or asking for estimates.

The DIY walkthrough below covers a cosmetic refresh of sound, dry surfaces. It does not teach plumbing, electrical, shower waterproofing, demolition of unknown materials, or structural repairs. You can manage the whole renovation while hiring qualified people for those portions.

## Scope checklist

Write an included / excluded / undecided list for the vanity, countertop, sink, faucet, toilet, tub or shower, wet-area walls, floor, mirror, lighting, ventilation, trim, accessories, and disposal. Mark each item KEEP, REFRESH, REPLACE IN PLACE, or RELOCATE. Relocation affects more than the visible fixture.

Example scope: keep the existing layout and toilet; replace the tub surround using a specified waterproofing system; repaint the dry walls; retain the vanity cabinet and replace its pulls. List who buys each item and who installs it. Do not describe all of this simply as 'remodel the bathroom.'

## Before you begin

Measure room length, width and ceiling height; record door swing, opening widths, vanity width, storage needs and the tub/shower footprint. Photograph the whole room and existing trouble spots. Do not open walls to get measurements.

Check that selected products physically fit and have compatible installation requirements. Decide where materials will be stored, how the work area will be isolated, and which toilet and shower the household can use during the work. Confirm delivery dates before agreeing to demolition.

## Safety and stopping points

Stop if you see active leaks, soft flooring, unexplained staining, recurring mold, damaged wiring, or suspect old flooring/adhesives. These need assessment before a cosmetic refresh. In older homes, disturbing painted surfaces can create lead dust; obtain appropriate assessment and qualified help before sanding or demolition. Do not scrape or sample suspect asbestos yourself.

Tile, grout and ordinary water-resistant board are not by themselves a complete shower-waterproofing system. Ask the installer to identify the complete assembly and verification steps. Keep plumbing, electrical, structural changes and wet-area waterproofing outside the DIY walkthrough.

## Budget: what to include

Build a budget by work package: investigation and protection; demolition/disposal; repairs; trade work; waterproofing and finishes; fixtures; permits/inspections; and cleanup. Add delivery, tax and temporary bathroom arrangements. Keep a separate reserve for concealed damage rather than assuming every unknown is in the base price.

Price allowances need a quantity and a dollar basis: for example, a tile purchase allowance per square foot is different from installed tile pricing. Ask what happens when the final selection exceeds the allowance. These are planning categories, not a San Antonio price quote.

## Tools and materials

Cosmetic-refresh kit: tape measure, camera, drop cloths, cleaning supplies compatible with the finish, painter's tape, suitable brushes/rollers, a stable step stool, screwdrivers, labeled bags for hardware, and PPE specified on product labels. Select coating and primer for the actual substrate and bathroom conditions.

Do not buy a demolition tool or wet-area system until the scope and professional responsibilities are settled. Product instructions determine preparation, ventilation, recoating and return-to-service times.

## Common mistakes

Ordering a vanity before measuring the door and plumbing constraints; tiling over a moisture problem; treating grout as waterproofing; leaving demolition, substrate repair or disposal out of the estimate; and scheduling custom glass before final dimensions are confirmed can all turn a simple budget into a series of changes.

## DIY suitability

A cosmetic refresh may fit a homeowner comfortable with careful surface preparation, painting and reusing existing hardware holes. Start only with sound, dry surfaces and safe access. The steps below deliberately leave fixture connections, wet areas and hidden-condition repairs to professionals.

## When to hire help

Hire help for a shower rebuild, fixture relocation, damaged subfloor, electrical changes, ventilation changes, or uncertainty about moisture. You can still handle selections, recordkeeping, and some finishing tasks if those responsibilities are agreed before work starts.

## Choose the version of the project

Cosmetic refresh: retain fixtures and layout; update dry-area paint and compatible hardware. Replace in place: new fixtures or finishes in their existing locations, with professional connections and any required permits. Full renovation: open assemblies, rebuild wet areas or change the layout. Ask for separate estimates when comparing fundamentally different scopes.

If accessibility matters, list the actual need—safer entry, easier cleaning, reachable storage, or room for assistance—before choosing fixtures. Have a qualified designer or installer verify clearances and support for any safety equipment.

## San Antonio checks before work begins

Confirm the property is inside San Antonio city limits and which authority issues permits. Cosmetic work and work involving trades are treated differently. Use Development Services' current residential-permit guidance to verify your exact scope, inspection sequence and contractor registration requirements. HOA approval is a separate question. Nearby cities and unincorporated areas may have different rules.

Ask who obtains permits and who schedules each inspection. A contractor's statement that a project is 'small' is not a permit determination.

## Expect work stages, waiting periods and bathroom downtime

A full renovation commonly moves through selections and ordering, protection, demolition, assessment of exposed conditions, rough trade work, required inspections, substrate/waterproofing work, finishes, fixtures and a final walkthrough. Some stages overlap; others cannot.

A cosmetic refresh can often be divided into short sessions. A full rebuild can leave the room unusable through multiple stages. Obtain a written schedule for your scope rather than relying on a universal number of days. Separate working days from delivery waits, inspection availability, drying/curing and glass fabrication. No one should promise a usable shower before the selected system permits it.

## DIY readiness: keep this a cosmetic refresh

Choose a small refresh only after confirming the room is dry and in sound condition. You should be able to protect the room, follow coating labels, work from safe footing, and stop when the surface or existing assembly is unfamiliar. Keep another bathroom available when possible.

Before buying: count existing cabinet pulls, measure their hole spacing, identify the wall/cabinet finish, and read the chosen coating system's directions. Avoid tasks requiring new holes near concealed utilities. Photograph the starting condition and write down what you are leaving untouched.

## 1. Plan the refresh and stage the room

Choose the dry-wall color and any same-hole replacement pulls. Create a small shopping list and check product compatibility. Test the proposed finish on an inconspicuous approved area. Remove toiletries, towels and loose accessories; protect fixtures and floors without blocking ventilation or creating a trip hazard. Set aside labeled bags for parts.

Checkpoint: you can explain what will change and what will remain, and no leak or suspect material needs disturbing.

## 2. Clean and inspect before coating

Use a cleaner appropriate to the surface and coating directions; never mix cleaning chemicals. Allow surfaces to dry as directed. Inspect after cleaning. If staining returns, the surface stays damp, paint is failing for an unknown reason, or a wall feels soft, pause and obtain an assessment. Do not hide damage with primer.

Checkpoint: the dry surface is sound and ready for the preparation method specified by the selected product. Older or unknown coatings require hazard assessment before abrasion.

## 3. Protect edges and apply the selected finish

Mask and protect the areas staying in place. Use the coating manufacturer's preparation, primer, application and ventilation instructions for the actual surface. Maintain safe footing and keep children and pets outside the work area. Wait the stated interval between coats; a dry-looking surface may not be ready for another coat or normal bathroom use.

Checkpoint: coverage is even, adjacent surfaces are protected, and the coating's required cure and use restrictions are recorded.

## 4. Refit compatible hardware and restore the room

When finishes are ready, replace cabinet pulls using the existing compatible holes and fasteners; avoid overtightening or damaging the cabinet face. If a new part does not fit, stop rather than enlarging holes or improvising near concealed services. Restore loose accessories and remove protection carefully. No plumbing or electrical connections are included in this step.

Checkpoint: doors and drawers operate normally, nothing is loose, and paint and packaging are handled as the product labels and local disposal rules require.

## 5. Check, document and maintain

Check the finish in normal lighting and note any touchups. Photograph the completed room, save paint codes, product labels, receipts and care instructions, and note when normal cleaning and use can resume. Continue watching for moisture or stains; cosmetic improvement does not fix a hidden leak.

Need help partway through? Record the exact unfinished task, what you used, photos and the problem you encountered. Share that context when requesting professional assistance instead of restarting the description.

## Give every contractor the same scope

Use your keep/replace/relocate list and the same selections or allowances for every estimate. Request line items for demolition, disposal, hidden-condition investigation, trade work, waterproofing, tile preparation/installation, fixtures, paint, permits and cleanup. Ask what is expressly excluded and what must be inspected before a price can be confirmed.

For a shower, ask for the named waterproofing system, drain/interface details, treatment of penetrations and verification required before it is covered. Ask how photos, required inspections and product documentation will be retained.

## Questions to ask before hiring

Who supervises each day, and which portions are subcontracted? Who performs plumbing and electrical work, and how can I verify the applicable credentials? What insurance applies? May I see comparable completed work and speak with recent customers?

Who supplies fixtures, verifies compatibility, stores deliveries, protects the home, handles a damaged delivery, and cleans each day? How will toilet/shower downtime be handled? What happens if damage appears after demolition, and who authorizes changes before the added work proceeds?

## Turn the estimate into clear expectations

Record scope, exclusions, selections/allowances, access, approximate work sequence and payment terms in writing. Tie progress reviews to observable deliverables, not just calendar dates. Ask what any deposit covers and avoid paying the entire project price before the work is performed.

Use written change approval that shows the extra work, price and schedule effect. MyHomeBro can help organize the scope, milestones and records; available payment options depend on the project and agreement. A platform workflow does not guarantee workmanship or an outcome.

## Final walkthrough: function, finish and records

Walk through with the contractor when the room is ready for its intended use. Check doors/drawers, fixture operation, visible leaks, drainage, ventilation, finish damage and the agreed accessory installation. Have the contractor demonstrate systems; a homeowner walkthrough does not replace required inspections or professional waterproofing verification.

List remaining items with owners and target dates. Collect inspection records where applicable, product and care information, warranties, labeled paint/finish details and agreed project photos. Follow the agreement's completion and payment process; resolve open items in writing.

**Build my DIY plan:** after publication, create an editable project from the five scoped DIY steps. Sign in or create an account to save it.

**Plan my contractor request:** begin a guide-linked request, with project details collected before contact information. Starting a request does not send contractor invitations.

## Questions homeowners ask

**Should I buy the fixtures before hiring?**

Make selections early, but have the installer verify dimensions, compatibility, lead times and responsibilities before you place nonreturnable orders.

**Can I do the painting and hire someone for the rest?**

Yes, when the scope, sequence, surface readiness and responsibility for damage or rework are agreed in advance. Keep wet-area, trade and hidden-condition work with qualified professionals.

**How much will my bathroom renovation cost?**

A reliable estimate needs your scope, location, conditions and selections. Compare matching written work packages and allowances; the cheapest total may leave important work out.

**Is grout a waterproofing system?**

No. Ask for the complete wet-area assembly appropriate to the chosen products and the verification required before covering it.

# Plan a backyard shed that fits your yard, storage and budget

Plan a backyard storage shed, check San Antonio permit exemptions, follow a small shed-kit DIY workflow, and compare complete installation estimates.

## What this project involves

A useful shed starts with what goes inside: equipment, bins, door clearance and room to move. The kit price may exclude the base, delivery, anchors, tools and help with assembly. Compare the complete usable result before buying.

A small storage-shed kit can be a DIY project when its instructions, site conditions, lifting requirements and your experience fit. This walkthrough organizes that process around the exact model's manual. It is not a custom structural design. A shed intended as a bedroom, business space or dwelling needs separate review.

## Scope checklist

Record the exterior footprint, usable interior space, door clear opening, door swing, threshold, intended loads, ventilation and storage layout. Identify who handles site preparation, support/base, delivery, assembly, anchoring, weatherproofing, finishes and cleanup.

Example: a 10-by-12-foot detached storage shed is 120 square feet. That area falls below San Antonio's shed exemption threshold, but does not settle its placement, construction or utility requirements. Choose a model with a complete assembly manual and a support/anchoring arrangement suitable for the actual site. A DIY shell with a professionally prepared base is also an option.

## Before you begin

Photograph the proposed site after rain, nearby structures, fences, trees and the delivery route. Find property-line and easement information rather than assuming the fence is the boundary. Measure the route and opening heights as well as widths. Check equipment turning and unloading space with the supplier.

Keep emergency access, drainage paths and required utility access clear. Before any digging, arrange Texas811 notification and investigate private lines that may not be covered. Do not move markers or begin excavation just because a sketch looks clear.

## Safety and stopping points

Permit exemption does not establish structural safety or DIY readiness. Follow the exact kit's support, assembly, temporary bracing, anchoring and weather instructions. Plan the specified crew and lifting method before opening large panels; stop for wind, unstable parts or work beyond your safe reach. Never stand on shed roof panels or improvise lifting equipment.

Do not alter framing, substitute connectors, omit anchors or cover a soft floor. Get qualified help for uncertain soils, drainage, custom structural details, unsupported loads or assembly you cannot safely control. Use appropriate professionals for utility installations; do not improvise a permanent power supply with an extension cord.

## Budget: what to include

Separate the shell price from the installed price: site assessment; access preparation; specified support/base; delivery and assembly; anchoring; roofing/weatherproofing; finish; permit costs; and storage equipment. Add any professional utility work as a separate scope. Difficult delivery, poor drainage, heavy storage loads or extensive site work can change the price materially.

Compare the same usable size and door arrangement. A cheaper kit may omit a floor or have a door too narrow for your equipment. Keep a reserve for site conditions that cannot be priced from the brochure.

## Tools and materials

Start with the exact model's manual and parts list, then assemble only the tools it specifies. Common planning and assembly tools may include a tape measure, level, square, screwdrivers, drill/driver and compatible bits, clamps, labeled fastener containers and eye/hand protection. Manual-specific access equipment and helpers are part of the plan, not optional extras.

Confirm whether anchors, floor components, sealants, finishes and tools are included. A kit's floor panels do not necessarily provide the required foundation. Select support and anchoring for the stated model, ground conditions and intended loads; do not borrow dimensions or anchor details from another kit.

## Common mistakes

Buying from outside dimensions alone; failing to check the door opening and threshold; treating delivery as assembly; placing a shed in an easement or drainage path; assuming a small shed has no location restrictions; and loading unverified wall shelving are common ways the final project differs from the advertised kit.

## DIY suitability

A small shed kit can be suitable DIY work with a model-specific manual, a verified support/base and anchoring arrangement, manageable lifting and the required helpers. You may split the job: hire site/base work or difficult assembly, then complete ground-access finishing and storage yourself. Permit status is a separate check from skill and safety.

## When to hire help

Hire help for unsuitable or uncertain ground, significant grading, custom structural design, difficult lifting or work at height, utility connections, or tasks beyond your tools and experience. A permit requirement does not automatically rule out homeowner construction; confirm the applicable homeowner-permit process and inspection responsibilities with Development Services.

## San Antonio shed permits: check area and conditions

Within San Antonio city limits, the published building-permit exemption covers one-story detached tool/storage sheds and similar accessory structures with a floor area of 300 square feet or less, provided applicable setbacks are met. Exactly 300 square feet is included in that threshold. A 10-by-12-foot shed is 120 square feet; a 12-by-24-foot shed is 288. Confirm how the city measures your proposed structure.

This does not remove zoning, easement, drainage, district or HOA restrictions. Electrical, plumbing and mechanical work can require separate permits. Altered shipping containers and garages have different rules. Check the actual address and intended use before ordering; a San Antonio mailing address may be outside city jurisdiction. Above the exemption threshold, ask about permits rather than assuming DIY is prohibited.

## Size for what goes inside

Draw the mower, tool storage and work area to scale, with a clear route between them and the door. Use the delivered interior dimensions. Check shelf depth, overhead clearance, ramp/threshold needs and where long items will turn.

List heavy items individually and ask the supplier to confirm the floor/support arrangement for the intended loads. Treat fuel, chemicals and battery charging as separate storage/safety questions; follow product and equipment instructions rather than assuming all belongings can be stored together.

## What a realistic sequence looks like

Resolve location and approvals, choose the design, confirm access and lead time, prepare the specified support system, complete any required checks, then deliver/assemble and finish. A prepared kit can arrive quickly while site preparation or approvals take longer. Obtain separate dates for site readiness, delivery, installation and usable storage.

Rain, wet ground, supplier rescheduling or an unsuitable delivery route can delay the job. Ask who protects materials and who pays for a failed delivery when the route was not ready.

## DIY readiness: choose a kit you can safely assemble

Read the complete assembly manual before purchase, including base, anchoring, weather limits, crew size, roof assembly and access requirements. Decide which stages you can perform and which need help. Do not proceed without an applicable support/anchoring solution and a practical way to handle every component safely.

This walkthrough is for a small detached storage-shed kit built to its instructions. Custom framing, structural modifications, habitable conversions and utility installation need a separate plan. If the manual requires equipment, helpers or skills you do not have, arrange them or hire that stage.

## 1. Confirm the location, model and scope

Sketch property boundaries, easements, drainage, nearby structures and the proposed footprint. Verify the permit and approval position for the address. Measure the access route, stored equipment and door clear opening. Read the model's floor-load, support and anchoring requirements before ordering.

Checkpoint: the site, intended use, model, approvals and division of DIY/hired work are documented. Save the manual and model number with the project.

## 2. Prepare and verify the specified base

Before digging, notify Texas811 within its required notice period and identify private lines that may need a separate locator. Follow the selected model's base details and address actual ground, drainage and load conditions. Hire preparation or foundation work if those conditions are uncertain.

Check the required dimensions, level, squareness, support and anchor provisions before assembling the shell. Do not compensate for an unsuitable site with improvised stacks of blocks.

Checkpoint: the base meets the documented requirements, applicable checks are complete, and the support and anchoring solution is ready.

## 3. Inventory and assemble in the manual’s sequence

Inventory panels, framing, hardware and connectors against the parts list. Label similar pieces and keep fasteners separated. Set up the required helpers, tools and safe work area. Assemble floor, walls and roof components in the exact model's sequence, using its temporary stabilization and fastening instructions.

Do not leave an unstable partial shell unattended, force misaligned panels or climb onto the roof. Pause for missing parts, unsafe weather or lifting beyond the planned crew.

Checkpoint: each stage matches the manual before the next stage hides its connections. Photograph progress without interrupting safe support of components.

## 4. Complete anchoring, doors and weather protection

Install the specified anchoring, remaining connectors, doors and weather-protection components using the approved arrangement for this model and base. Do not guess anchor type, spacing or capacity. Obtain supplier or qualified professional guidance when those details are unresolved.

Check door operation, visible gaps, hardware and the manual's completion checklist. Keep vents and drainage openings clear. If coating is permitted, use compatible products and their application/drying instructions.

Checkpoint: support, assembly and anchoring requirements are complete; movement, binding doors, damage or leaks are resolved before loading.

## 5. Add storage and keep an inspection record

Install storage according to its instructions and the shed's capacity limits. Put heavier items low. Do not suspend loads or anchor shelving to unverified wall locations. Keep the door route clear. Save model/manual, approvals, base and anchoring details, photos, receipts and warranties.

Recheck after rain and seasonally for leaks, settling, loose components, pests and door changes. Stop using affected areas if movement, damage or a soft floor develops; arrange assessment rather than covering the problem.

## Ask for a complete installed estimate

Have the supplier or contractor identify the building model/specification, support system, installation responsibility, anchoring, roof/weatherproofing, finish, delivery conditions and access requirements. List removals, site work, utilities and permits separately. Confirm whether the base must be complete before delivery and who verifies it.

Ask for written responsibility when the installer, base contractor and shed supplier are different businesses. A gap between their scopes can leave the homeowner coordinating rework.

## Questions for the supplier and installer

Who evaluates ground and drainage conditions? What floor loads and anchoring assumptions apply? Who performs delivery and assembly, and what insurance covers their work? What happens if access fails, parts are missing or the base does not meet the kit requirements?

Who obtains approvals and arranges checks? What work is excluded from the quoted price? How is installation workmanship covered separately from the building materials, and whom do I call if a door binds after the first heavy rain?

## Accept the building and keep the records

Check the selected model and dimensions, doors/latches, visible weather sealing, agreed finish, site cleanup and any accessories. Have the installer explain the support/anchoring and maintenance requirements; visual inspection alone is not a structural certification.

Record incomplete items, owners and correction dates. Retain applicable approvals, installation records, product instructions and warranties. Follow the written agreement's review and payment terms.

## Questions homeowners ask

**What is San Antonio’s shed building-permit exemption?**

The published exemption covers qualifying one-story detached storage sheds of 300 square feet or less that meet applicable setbacks. Other location, use, district and utility requirements still apply. Confirm the proposal for the actual address.

**Can I turn a storage shed into a guest room later?**

Do not assume so. Habitable use changes the project. Discuss zoning, building, fire, sanitation and utility requirements with the applicable authority before designing or buying.

**Should I buy a kit or hire a builder?**

Compare the complete installed scope, site suitability, access, warranty responsibilities and intended use. Either option needs a suitable support and anchoring arrangement.

**Can I build the shed myself?**

A suitable small kit may be a DIY project when you can follow its complete instructions, provide suitable support and anchoring, and handle assembly safely with the required helpers. Permit requirements and DIY suitability are separate decisions.

# Plan, build or refresh a deck with clear expectations

Check San Antonio deck permit conditions, plan a low detached DIY deck from a complete design, compare contractor estimates, and care for an existing deck.

## What this project involves

A deck is an outdoor structure with supports, connections and a walking surface that must work together. Start by choosing your route: build a small low detached deck, hire a new deck or repairs, or maintain a sound existing deck.

The DIY build workflow below covers a simple uncovered detached deck using a complete site-appropriate design. It organizes construction decisions and checkpoints; it does not supply universal beam spans, footing dimensions or connector schedules. Permit exemption does not remove construction requirements, and a permit requirement does not automatically prohibit homeowner construction.

## Scope checklist

Describe the intended use, approximate footprint, height above ground, connection to the house, doors served, stairs, guards, shade, lighting and furniture. Include any unusually heavy use, such as a hot tub, for professional design review rather than adding it later.

For a refresh, separate cleaning/finishing from replacement of boards or structural components. A quote to 'replace the decking' may exclude the supports, connectors, flashing, guards and disposal. Have the contractor assess what can actually be retained before comparing prices.

## Before you begin

Photograph the deck and accessible areas from safe ground-level positions. Note movement, damaged boards, staining near house connections and any loose guard or stair. Do not crawl beneath an unsafe structure or test a questionable guard by pushing it.

For a new deck, sketch doors, thresholds, yard grade, drainage, utilities, access and intended furniture. Check property restrictions and obtain a professional assessment before committing to a footprint or ordering materials. Notify Texas811 before any digging; ask about private lines as well.

For a DIY build, obtain a complete design covering the actual dimensions, soil/support conditions, lumber species and grade, spans, connections and any bracing, guards or steps. The city references adopted residential deck provisions and AWC DCA 6; confirm which provisions apply to this design. Do not combine unrelated internet diagrams into a structural plan.

## Safety and stopping points

Keep people off a deck that moves, sags, has damaged supports, loose guards or unsafe stairs, and obtain a qualified assessment. Do not hide deterioration with a coating or new surface boards. Stay off ladders and roof structures when performing the cleaning walkthrough.

Use cleaners and coatings appropriate to the actual material and follow their labels. Never mix chemicals. Protect people, pets, plants and surrounding surfaces. Avoid electrical equipment around wet conditions unless its instructions and protection are suitable. Stop if safe access or drainage cannot be maintained.

During a new build, control access to excavations and unfinished framing, use the planned temporary support/bracing, follow tool instructions and arrange manageable lifting. Avoid overhead utilities. Never use an unfinished or unstable frame as a work platform. Stop if actual site conditions or supplied materials differ from the design.

## Budget: what to include

Ask for separate costs for design/approvals; removals; site/support work; framing/connections; surface boards; guards/stairs; finishes; cleanup and any trade work. Material selection alone does not explain the installed price. Access, height, poor existing conditions and complex stairs can matter substantially.

For maintenance, count cleaner/finish, applicators, protection, disposal and tool rental as well as material coverage and coats. Measure first and use the selected product's coverage guidance. Compare the same proposed scope and list unresolved existing conditions explicitly.

## Tools and materials

For safe surface care: broom, hose, soft brush suitable for the product, compatible cleaner, buckets, label-specified PPE, protective coverings and suitable applicators if finishing is permitted. Keep a clear route on and off the deck.

Do not treat a rented pressure washer as a universal deck-cleaning tool. Manufacturers differ, and some composite products can be damaged by pressure washing or cannot be recoated. Identify the exact material and care instructions before choosing equipment.

For a new build, use the design’s material and connector schedule to make the shopping list. Typical tools include measuring and layout tools, a level and square, appropriate saw/drill equipment, clamps, PPE and task-specific digging/compaction tools. Confirm experience with each tool and utility clearance before excavation. Select fasteners and hardware for the actual treated lumber and exposure; ordinary interior screws are not a substitute for specified structural connectors.

## Common mistakes

Covering an unsafe deck with new boards; assuming guards or house connections are included in a surface quote; using the same cleaner or pressure setting on every material; trapping debris between boards; and applying finish before the substrate meets the coating requirements can cause avoidable problems.

## DIY suitability

A small low detached deck may be suitable for a homeowner with relevant carpentry skills, appropriate tools and a complete site-appropriate plan. Verify support, soil, connections, materials and safe access before starting. Cleaning and compatible finish maintenance are a separate DIY route for a sound existing deck.

## When to hire help

Get qualified help for an unclear design, uncertain soils or load capacity, unsafe existing structure, house attachment/flashing, elevated or complex work, stairs/guards beyond your experience, roof covers, hot tubs or other concentrated loads. Arrange appropriate professionals and permits for utility work. You can hire design or supports and complete suitable portions yourself.

## San Antonio deck permits: size is only one condition

For the residential uncovered-deck exemption, check every condition: area of 300 square feet or less; no point more than 30 inches above grade; no attachment to the dwelling; and no service to a required exit door. The city's residential improvements application identifies exceeding either size/height limit, house attachment or serving a required exit as permit triggers. Confirm the actual door and design with Development Services.

A 12-by-16-foot deck is 192 square feet, but still needs the other conditions checked. Measure height at the highest point above the actual ground beneath the deck, including sloping ground. Treat a cover or roof as a separate scope to verify. Setbacks, easements, district/HOA rules and separate trade permits can still apply. These thresholds determine the exemption; they do not determine who may build. Ask about homeowner permits and inspections when required.

## Compare the whole material system

Ask the contractor to explain the surface material, fasteners, connectors, compatibility with treated materials, gaps, edge details, guards and maintenance. Obtain care instructions for the exact product rather than a generic 'composite' or 'wood' label.

Discuss heat exposure, barefoot use, furniture, color variation, replacement-board availability and future maintenance. A product warranty and an installation/workmanship warranty are different promises. Record who is responsible for each.

## Set expectations for construction and maintenance

A new deck requires decisions and site/design checks before ordering, then site work, support/framing stages, required checks, surface/guards/stairs and handover. Weather, access and concealed deterioration can change the sequence. Get a schedule tied to the actual design and material availability.

A maintenance project needs a suitable weather window, cleaning and drying time, and any coating's return-to-use time. Plan several available sessions instead of assuming every cleaner or stain is a same-day job.

## DIY readiness: a small, low, detached deck

Choose a simple uncovered detached design without a hot tub, roof or unresolved access requirements. Verify every permit-exemption condition or arrange the required approvals. A required exit, slope or connection to the house can change the project even at a small footprint.

Before ordering, have a complete plan applicable to the site, including supports, lumber, spans, fastening and lateral stability. Use qualified design help if the plan or site is uncertain. This workflow does not tell you to use loose deck blocks, shallow supports or guessed member sizes simply because the deck is low. New stairs, guards and heavy loads need explicit design decisions.

## 1. Confirm the site, approvals and complete design

Measure the proposed footprint and height above ground at all relevant points. Identify doors served, property restrictions, drainage, utilities and any house connection. Record the permit decision for the actual scope and address.

Select a complete site-appropriate design and bill of materials, including support and connection details. Verify its limits before purchasing.

Checkpoint: the design, approvals, material schedule and division of DIY/professional tasks are resolved. No structural dimensions are being invented during assembly.

## 2. Locate utilities and install the planned supports

Notify Texas811 before digging and arrange location of private lines where necessary. Lay out the design with strings and measurements; verify dimensions, squareness, clearances and drainage. Install the specified support system only after site and soil suitability are established, following applicable approvals and inspection hold points.

Use help for concrete, excavation or support work beyond your ability. Do not alter footing dimensions or substitute support methods when an obstruction or poor soil appears.

Checkpoint: support locations, levels, anchorage and applicable checks match the plan before framing begins.

## 3. Assemble and check the framing

Confirm lumber grade, treatment, member sizes and connector compatibility against the plan. Build in its sequence with the specified temporary stabilization, connections and fasteners. Check alignment, level and squareness as you go; do not substitute hardware or enlarge spans to use available boards.

Keep people off incomplete framing and arrange manageable lifting. Resolve unexpected movement, splits, unsuitable materials or a design mismatch before covering connections.

Checkpoint: the support/framing and connections are checked against the design, photographed and inspected where required before decking conceals them.

## 4. Install decking and complete safe access

Install the selected decking using its product instructions together with the structural plan: fastening, gaps, end support and edge details must fit both. Finish all designed steps, guards, bracing and access provisions rather than assuming a low deck needs none. Keep unfinished areas closed to use.

If access requirements or material instructions conflict with the design, pause for clarification. Do not add a house ledger, roof, hot tub or heavy planter arrangement without reviewing the changed scope.

Checkpoint: walking surfaces, fasteners and access details are complete and checked against the plan and applicable requirements.

## 5. Check completion and establish maintenance

Complete required inspections and the plan/product completion checks before use. Resolve movement, sharp edges, protruding fasteners or unsafe access. Save the design, approvals, inspection records, materials, photos and product instructions. A homeowner walkthrough does not replace a required inspection.

Follow material-specific cleaning and coating guidance. Inspect periodically and after notable weather for movement, damage or loose components. Obtain qualified assessment for structural concerns; a new coating cannot repair them.

## Existing deck: confirm condition before cleaning

For a maintenance-only project, first confirm the deck is safe to occupy. Stay off a structure with movement, decay, damaged supports, loose guards or unsafe stairs and arrange assessment. Identify the exact board material and existing finish. Do not sand unknown old coatings or assume every composite can be coated.

## Protect, test and clean the surface

Clear movable items, protect surrounding surfaces and plants, and manage runoff according to the product label. Sweep loose debris. Test a manufacturer-approved cleaner and method in an inconspicuous area, then work in manageable sections with the specified contact time and rinse method. Never mix chemicals or assume stronger pressure is better.

## Dry, finish if permitted and record the work

Allow the surface to dry as instructed. Apply a compatible coating only if the decking manufacturer permits it and conditions meet its instructions. Follow safe disposal directions for finish-soaked materials and the return-to-use time. Record products and dates, keep accessible gaps clear using approved methods, and watch for new damage or movement.

## Specify the structure as well as the surface

Ask the contractor to document the complete build/repair scope, including supports, connections to the home where applicable, moisture/flashing details, guards/stairs, surface material and required checks. Identify which existing components will remain and the basis for accepting their condition.

List access, removals, delivery, disposal, utilities, yard restoration, finish and permits. Have heavy loads and special uses addressed in the design rather than relying on a general statement that the deck will be 'strong enough.'

## Questions before choosing a deck contractor

Who develops or verifies the design, and who performs the structural work? What similar work can I inspect or discuss with previous customers? What credentials, registration and insurance apply to this scope? Who coordinates required inspections and any specialty trades?

What conditions could change the price after removal begins? Who approves substitutions and changes? What should I expect for work-area protection, rain delays, daily access restrictions and cleanup?

## Final checks and first-season expectations

Have the contractor demonstrate safe access, stair/guard completion and the intended use restrictions. Review required inspection records, product instructions and warranty responsibilities. Record visible damage, missing items and agreed corrections. A homeowner walkthrough is not an engineering inspection.

Ask what normal material movement or appearance changes may occur, what is covered, and which symptoms require a prompt call. Follow the agreement's completion and payment process and retain photos and maintenance instructions.

## Questions homeowners ask

**Can new boards fix a weak deck?**

No. The supports, connections, guards and stairs need a qualified assessment. A better surface cannot compensate for an unsafe structure.

**Can I clean every deck with a pressure washer?**

No. Material and manufacturer instructions differ. Identify the product and approved method first; stronger pressure is not automatically better.

**How long should a new deck take?**

Ask for a schedule based on the design, approvals, material lead times and site conditions. Separate construction days from the total calendar time.

**Does every deck under 300 square feet avoid a permit?**

No. The exemption includes exactly 300 square feet but also requires an uncovered deck no more than 30 inches above grade at any point, detached from the dwelling and not serving a required exit door. Verify the full scope and local restrictions.

**Can I build a deck myself if a permit is required?**

Permit status is separate from DIY suitability. San Antonio provides a homeowner application route subject to its eligibility requirements. Confirm the current process, design and inspection responsibilities before starting.

# Plan a patio around drainage, daily use and the work below the surface

Compare concrete and pavers, write a complete installation scope, and follow a small ground-level paver-patio planning and DIY walkthrough with clear limits.

## What this project involves

Start with the way you want to use the patio: a table, grill area, seating, a path, or space for children to play. Mark the furniture and walking routes before choosing a finish. A patio that looks large on paper may still leave no room to pull out a chair.

The DIY route is limited to a small, uncovered, ground-level pedestrian paver area on a suitable site. It is not a foundation, driveway, retaining wall, drainage redesign or support for a roof, pool or hot tub. Concrete work, steep sites and complex drainage belong in a professional scope.

## Scope checklist

Define approximate dimensions, location, intended loads, finish, edges and transitions to doors, paths and lawn. List demolition, excavation, spoil disposal, utility locating, base preparation, drainage, surface installation and cleanup separately. Identify landscaping or irrigation that must be protected or professionally changed.

For concrete, ask the contractor to specify the design, thickness, reinforcement where required, joints, finish and curing arrangements for the actual use. For pavers, specify the selected system, site-appropriate base, edge restraint, bedding and joint treatment. Do not compare only the surface price per square foot.

## Before you begin

Observe the area after rain and photograph existing ponding and runoff routes. Check how doors and thresholds relate to the proposed finished level. Find property/easement information and utility records. Mark the intended layout without disturbing the soil, then confirm applicable approvals.

Arrange Texas811 notification before digging and verify how private utilities will be located. Protect utility access, drainage paths and the building's moisture-control details. Do not solve ponding by directing water toward a neighbor or a foundation.

## Safety and stopping points

Stop for unknown lines, major roots, unstable fill, steep ground, persistent ponding, foundation concerns or uncertain drainage. A utility notification is not permission to ignore marks or private services. Obtain professional advice before excavation in a complicated area.

Pavers and equipment are heavy; plan safe handling and follow equipment instructions. Dry cutting masonry or concrete can generate hazardous dust. Plan a no-cut layout or arrange controlled cutting by a qualified provider. Do not mix or place structural concrete, disturb a building foundation or construct retaining work from this guide.

## Budget: what to include

Price removals and disposal, excavation, base materials, compaction, edge restraints, the selected surface, joint treatment and delivery/rental costs. Add access constraints and any professional drainage or utility work. A poor base can make a low surface price expensive later.

For pavers, calculate rectangular area as length × width; split irregular layouts into simple shapes. Use the product layout and supplier's waste guidance rather than ordering exactly the visible area. For loose materials, use the specified installed area/depth and supplier conversion guidance; compacted quantities can differ from loose delivery quantities. These calculations help request a quote, not establish a structural design.

## Tools and materials

For a suitable small paver project: tape measure, layout markers, level/stringline equipment appropriate to the design, shovel, wheelbarrow, compatible base and bedding materials, edge restraints, the selected pavers, joint material and approved compaction equipment. Add gloves, eye/hearing protection and handling aids appropriate to the tasks.

Confirm tool availability, equipment transport and manufacturer instructions before ordering. Do not substitute an incompatible bedding or joint product because it is labeled 'sand.'

## Common mistakes

Installing over loose topsoil; changing levels without considering door thresholds and runoff; omitting edge restraints; using a universal base depth without assessing the site; ordering no allowance for the layout; and loading or cleaning a new surface before the product instructions permit it can undermine the finished result.

## DIY suitability

A small, simple paver area may suit a homeowner who can safely handle the work and follow a verified site-appropriate installation plan. Preparation and compaction are a large part of the work. Use a no-cut layout when possible and stop if drainage or ground conditions are uncertain.

## When to hire help

Hire help for a concrete slab, large or complex paver installation, steep/poorly drained ground, retaining edges, heavy loads, utility changes or patio covers. A roofed patio is a separate structural project, not an accessory to order after the slab is installed.

## Choose concrete or pavers for the actual use

Concrete can provide a continuous surface; pavers use individual units and a compatible base/edge system. Compare appearance, movement/repair expectations, cleaning, access and future changes. Ask who addresses cracking, settlement, drainage complaints or damaged units under the stated warranty.

Neither finish fixes unsuitable ground or a poor drainage plan. Discuss the construction below the finish with the installer before deciding by color or price alone.

## Check local requirements and utilities

Ask the authority for the actual address about the proposed patio, finished elevations and any attached or covered portion. Do not assume an exemption for one kind of paved area also covers a roof, utility installation, retaining structure or work in an easement. Check HOA and district restrictions separately.

Texas811 asks for notification at least two business days before digging, excluding weekends and holidays. Follow the ticket instructions and verify responses before work. Ask about locating private lines that utility members may not mark.

## Expect the preparation to take real time

Plan the layout and approvals, order materials, arrange locating, prepare the site, install the specified base and surface, then complete joints/edges and cleanup. Weather, wet ground, deliveries and access can stretch the calendar. Avoid promising a party-ready date before these dependencies are confirmed.

Paver work can involve several separate work sessions; concrete has its own placement and curing restrictions. Use product and installer directions for foot traffic, furniture and other loads rather than treating a hard-looking surface as fully ready.

## DIY readiness: choose a simple pedestrian paver area

Proceed only with a suitable site and an installation plan specifying finished levels, drainage and the appropriate materials. Keep the area small enough for safe handling, and arrange help for lifting or equipment. Do not start excavation until required locating and checks are complete.

If you cannot explain where water will go without harming the home or neighboring property, stop and hire help. This walkthrough does not choose a base depth, structural specification or drainage slope for your property.

## 1. Confirm the layout and material system

Lay out the intended perimeter, furniture and walking space without digging. Choose the paver format and a simple pattern, preferably avoiding cuts. Use the supplier's layout to identify quantities, matching edge restraints, bedding and joint materials.

Checkpoint: the patio fits the intended use and a site-appropriate plan defines the finished levels and assembly.

## 2. Complete locating and prepare the work area

Confirm utility responses and private-line arrangements, approvals and access before disturbing the soil. Keep people and pets out of the work area. Excavate only to the verified installation plan, manage spoil safely, and stop for unknown lines or unexpected ground conditions.

Checkpoint: utilities, excavation limits and material handling are resolved; no unplanned drainage or structural work is being improvised.

## 3. Install the specified base and edges

Follow the chosen system and verified site plan for base materials, lift thicknesses, compaction, levels and edge restraints. Recheck drainage and finished-height references as the work progresses. Do not cover an unstable or waterlogged area simply to keep moving.

Checkpoint: the base and edges meet the specified preparation requirements before bedding or pavers hide them.

## 4. Place pavers and finish the joints

Prepare the specified bedding and lay the selected pattern while checking alignment and finished levels. Use the required method for seating the pavers and treating joints; follow product-specific weather, moisture and cleanup requirements. Arrange qualified help for cuts rather than dry cutting masonry.

Checkpoint: edges restrain the units, the surface is stable, and joints/cleanup follow the product directions.

## 5. Inspect, document and maintain

Keep traffic and furniture off until permitted by the selected system. Check accessible finished edges and transitions; note loose units, uneven areas or ponding that need assessment. Save material names, spare units, photos and care instructions. Monitor after rain and address movement rather than repeatedly hiding it with more joint material.

If you need help, record the installation materials, known base preparation, photos and the exact area of concern. Do not dig near marked services to diagnose it yourself.

## Compare complete installation estimates

Give each contractor the same dimensions, use, access and finish requirements. Ask what site investigation is included and how base preparation, excavated material disposal, drainage, joints/edges and cleanup are priced. Request written exclusions, allowances and responsibility for irrigation or landscaping damage.

For concrete, discuss finish variation, joints, curing/use restrictions and realistic cracking expectations. For pavers, discuss settlement, replacement and maintenance. A promise of a permanently perfect surface is less useful than a specific specification and correction policy.

## Questions before hiring

What similar installations can I see? Who evaluates the ground and drainage? Who specifies the assembly for the intended loads? What registration, insurance and specialty credentials apply? Who arranges locating, approvals and any required checks?

How will you protect the building, utility access and neighboring property? What can change after excavation, and how do I approve that change? Who is responsible for weather protection, material storage and safe access during construction?

## Walk the finished patio before closing out

Review dimensions, agreed finish, stable transitions, edges, cleanup and visible drainage behavior with the installer. Check required records and restrictions before putting furniture, grills or other loads on the patio. A walkthrough is not a substitute for engineering or required inspections.

Record any unresolved cracks, settlement, damaged units or ponding with the contractor and agree on the response. Keep the specification, care instructions, applicable approvals, warranty terms and final photos with the home record.
